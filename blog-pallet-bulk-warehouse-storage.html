Pallet and Bulk Storage 101: How Warehouses Maximize Capacity

October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

When a warehouse quotes you a rate, the number behind it is capacity: how many pallets fit in the building, and how much of that space your goods will take. Understanding how that gets calculated tells you whether a quote is fair, why one pallet storage warehouse costs more than another, and how to avoid paying for room you never use. Here is how it works, from a facility that has been doing this in Las Vegas for more than two decades.

Pallet Storage vs. Bulk Storage

Almost every warehouse uses both. They solve different problems, and which one your goods belong in has a direct effect on what you pay.

Pallet Storage

Goods are loaded onto pallets and placed into steel racking, one pallet per position. Every pallet stays individually accessible, so a forklift can pull position 14 without touching the thirteen around it. This suits inventory that moves regularly, needs rotation, or has to be picked in parts.

Bulk Storage

Goods are stacked directly on the floor, often several pallets high, with no racking between them. Bulk warehouse storage is cheaper per unit because you are not paying for steel, but you lose selective access: reaching the back of a stack means moving the front of it. Bulk suits high volume, low turnover inventory that ships as a block. Our warehouse services cover both, and we will tell you which your goods actually belong in rather than defaulting to the more expensive option.

How Warehouse Storage Capacity Is Actually Measured

Square footage is the number most people ask for, and it is the least useful one on its own. Warehouses measure warehouse storage capacity in pallet positions, because a building's real capacity depends on what it can stack, not what it covers.

Rack Height Multiplies Floor Space

A warehouse with 20 foot racking holds far more than one with 10 foot racking over the same footprint. Vertical space is the cheapest capacity a facility has, which is why floor-to-ceiling racking is standard in purpose built warehouses and rare in converted buildings.

Aisle Width Costs You Capacity

Every aisle is floor space holding nothing. Wider aisles mean faster forklift movement and easier access, narrower aisles mean more pallet positions. Facilities trade one against the other, and the right balance depends on how often goods move rather than on maximizing the count.

Weight and Load Limits

Racking has engineered load limits that cap what can sit on each beam regardless of available space. These are not arbitrary: the Rack Manufacturers Institute, which authors the ANSI MH16.1 standard for industrial pallet racks, sets the design and safety requirements that govern how racking is built and loaded. A facility that cannot tell you its load ratings is a facility worth asking more questions about.

What This Means for Your Costs

Three things follow directly from how capacity works, and all three affect your invoice.

  • Pallet count beats square footage when comparing quotes. Ask how many positions your goods will occupy, not how many square feet.
  • Badly built pallets waste capacity. Overhang, uneven stacking, and inconsistent heights all cost positions you still pay for.
  • Turnover decides the system. Fast moving goods in bulk storage create handling costs that erase the cheaper rate.

If you are comparing facilities, the International Warehouse Logistics Association maintains a directory of vetted third party warehouse providers along with the standards its members are expected to document.

When Storage Is Not the Answer

Sometimes the cheapest capacity is none at all. If your freight arrives and needs to leave again within days, paying for pallet positions is paying for dwell time you do not need. Cross docking moves goods from the inbound truck to the outbound one with minimal handling, which is why high turnover shippers often use it alongside, rather than instead of, a storage footprint.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is warehouse storage capacity measured?

Usually in pallet positions rather than square footage, since rack height and aisle width determine how much a given floor area can actually hold.

What is the difference between pallet storage and bulk storage?

Pallet storage uses racking so each pallet stays individually accessible. Bulk storage stacks goods on the floor, which costs less per unit but means moving the front to reach the back.

How many pallets fit in a typical warehouse bay?

It depends on rack height, beam spacing, and aisle width, so there is no universal figure. Ask any facility for its pallet position count rather than its square footage.
